Warning signs

When your engine asks for fresh oil.

Today’s oils last longer than they used to, but not forever. If you notice any of these, it’s time for a service.

Red oil pressure light? That’s not a reminder. It means oil pressure is too low. Pull over when it’s safe, shut the engine off and check the level. Driving on can damage the engine quickly, so call us before going further.

  • The oil pressure warning light comes on or flickers
  • Your maintenance reminder or windshield sticker says you’re due
  • Oil on the dipstick is low, gritty or thick and sludgy
  • Ticking or a louder-than-usual engine, especially at startup
  • A burning-oil smell or bluish smoke from the exhaust
  • Fresh oil spots on the driveway where you park
  • You’re topping off oil between changes

Oil types at a glance

Which oil does your engine need?

The short answer: whatever your owner’s manual lists. Here’s what the choices on the shelf actually mean.

The basics

Conventional

Refined from crude oil. It costs the least and works well in many older, simpler engines that call for it.

Good fit for Older engines, light everyday driving

Middle ground

Synthetic blend

Conventional oil mixed with synthetic base oils for better heat and cold protection than conventional alone, at a modest price bump.

Good fit for Engines that call for it, or a step up from conventional

Many newer engines

Full synthetic

Engineered for stability. It resists breaking down under heat, flows fast on cold starts and usually lasts longer between changes.

Good fit for Turbocharged and newer engines, extreme temperatures

Higher mileage

High-mileage

Contains seal conditioners and extra additives that can help older engines with minor seepage or oil consumption.

Good fit for Engines past roughly 75,000 miles

0W-20How to read the numbers

“0W” is the cold number

How easily the oil flows on a cold start. The W stands for winter, and a lower number flows faster.

“20” is the hot number

How thick the oil stays once the engine is at operating temperature. A higher number is thicker.

Your viscosity comes from the owner’s manual, and on many vehicles it’s printed right on the oil filler cap. Many newer engines require full synthetic in a specific grade, and that’s exactly what we put in.

Maintenance FAQ

Oil & maintenance, answered.

Not sure what your car is due for? Call with your year, make, model and mileage, and we’ll look it up.

Good to know: keep every maintenance receipt, whether we do the work or you do it yourself. Dated receipts showing the oil and parts used are the simplest proof that your car was maintained on schedule.

How often should I change my oil?

Follow your owner’s manual or your vehicle’s oil-life monitor. Many newer vehicles running synthetic oil go 5,000 to 10,000 miles between changes, while older engines and severe driving (lots of short trips, towing, dusty roads or extreme heat and cold) call for shorter intervals. Not sure what applies to you? We’ll look up the schedule for your vehicle.

Will having maintenance done here void my new-car warranty?

Generally, no. In the U.S., federal law says a manufacturer can’t void your warranty just because routine maintenance was done at an independent shop or by you. Follow the maintenance schedule, use oil and parts that meet the manufacturer’s specifications, and keep your receipts as a record.

Is synthetic oil worth it?

Full synthetic resists breaking down under heat, flows better on cold starts and usually lasts longer between changes. Conventional oil costs less and works well in many older, simpler engines. If your manual calls for synthetic, that’s what your engine needs. If it doesn’t, we’ll help you weigh the cost against how you drive.

What does the maintenance light mean, and can you reset it?

A maintenance or oil-life reminder is a schedule prompt based on mileage or the car’s own oil-life calculation, not a fault. We reset it at every oil service. A red oil pressure light or a check engine light is different: those point to a problem that should be checked.

Do I need an oil change if I don’t drive much?

Usually, yes. Oil breaks down with time as well as miles, and short trips never let the engine get hot enough to burn off moisture. That’s why most manufacturers list a time limit as well as a mileage limit, often six months to a year, whichever comes first.
