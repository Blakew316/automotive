Warning signs

Catch it before it gets expensive.

Engines and cooling systems usually give warnings well before a major failure. Acting early often means a smaller repair.

Temperature gauge climbing? Pull over when it’s safe and shut the engine off. Running hot can warp the cylinder head or damage the head gasket. Never open a hot radiator cap.

  • Temperature gauge climbing toward hot, or steam from under the hood
  • A bright green, orange or pink puddle, or a sweet, syrupy smell
  • Thick white exhaust smoke that won’t clear, or blue-gray smoke
  • Oil spots where you park, or a burning-oil smell after driving
  • A deep knock, or ticking and tapping from the engine
  • Rough running, misfires or a noticeable loss of power
  • Oil pressure warning light on while driving

Belt or chain?

Know what keeps your engine in time.

Most engines use a timing belt or a timing chain to keep the crankshaft and camshafts in step. Which one you have changes how you maintain it.

Scheduled replacement

Timing belt

A toothed, reinforced rubber belt that wears out with age and miles.

  • Replaced at a set interval, often somewhere around 60,000 to 105,000 miles, and sometimes by age
  • On many engines, a snapped belt lets the valves and pistons collide, which is a costly repair
  • Often replaced along with the water pump and tensioner when they share the same labor
No set interval

Timing chain

A metal chain lubricated by engine oil, designed to last much longer.

  • Usually no set replacement interval, and many last the life of the engine
  • Clean oil changed on time is the best way to protect it
  • A rattle at startup or certain check engine codes can mean a stretched chain or worn guides

Not sure which one your engine has? Check your owner’s manual, or ask us when you book.

What we do

Engine and cooling repair, done carefully.

Misfires & ignition

Spark plugs, ignition coils and fuel injectors tested cylinder by cylinder, then replaced as needed.

Oil leaks & gaskets

Timing belts & chains

Cooling system repair

Water pumps, radiators, thermostats, hoses and cooling fans, with a pressure test to confirm there are no leaks.

Overheating diagnosis

We find out why it runs hot, from low coolant or a stuck thermostat to a failed fan or a leaking head gasket.

Noises & engine testing

Knocks, ticks and lost power checked with compression and leak-down tests that show what’s happening inside.

Engine FAQ

Engine questions, answered.

Hearing something new or seeing a puddle? Call and describe it. We’ll tell you whether it’s safe to drive in.

Good to know: check your coolant level about once a month with the engine cold, using the marks on the reservoir. Low coolant is one of the most common causes of overheating, and catching it early can save your engine.

What should I do if my engine overheats?

Turn off the A/C and set the heater to full hot, which pulls some heat away from the engine. Then pull over as soon as it’s safe and shut the engine off. Never open a hot radiator cap or coolant reservoir, because pressurized coolant can cause serious burns. Let it cool and call us. Driving while overheating can warp the cylinder head or damage the head gasket.

How often does a timing belt need to be replaced?

It depends on the engine. Many manufacturers call for replacement somewhere around 60,000 to 105,000 miles, and some also set a limit in years. Your owner’s manual lists the interval for your vehicle. Engines with a timing chain usually have no set replacement interval. If you’re not sure which you have, ask us.

What does white smoke from the exhaust mean?

Thin white vapor on a cold morning is usually condensation and clears up as the engine warms. Thick white smoke that keeps coming, especially with a sweet smell or a dropping coolant level, can mean coolant is burning inside the engine, often from a leaking head gasket. Have it checked soon and keep an eye on your temperature gauge. Blue-gray smoke usually means the engine is burning oil.

Is a small oil leak urgent?

Usually it’s not an emergency, but don’t ignore it. Small leaks tend to grow, they can lower your oil level, and oil dripping onto hot exhaust parts can smoke or become a fire risk. Check your dipstick regularly and book an inspection. We’ll find where it’s coming from and tell you how soon it needs attention.

Should I repair my engine or replace it?

It depends on the damage, the vehicle’s overall condition and how long you plan to keep it. After testing, we explain what failed, what the repair involves and what it would cost, so you can weigh it against the vehicle’s value and your plans. The decision is always yours.
